Hemolysis caused by flow-induced mechanical damage to 
red blood cells is still a problem in medical devices such 
as ventricular assist devices (VADs), artificial lungs, and 
mechanical heart valves. A number of different models have 
been proposed by different research groups for calculating 
the hemolysis, and of these, the power law–based models 
(HI(%)Ctατ β) have proved the most popular because of 
their ease of use and applicability to a wide range of devices. 
However, within this power law category of models there 
are a number of different implementations. The aim of this 
work was to evaluate different power law–based models by 
calculating hemolysis in a specifically designed shearing 
device and a clinical VAD, and comparing the estimated 
results with experimental measurements of the hemolysis in 
these two devices. Both the Eulerian scalar transport and all 
the Lagrangian models had fairly large percentage of errors 
compared with the experiments (minimum Eulerian 91% 
and minimum Lagrangian 57%) showing they could not 
accurately predict the magnitude of the hemolysis. How -
ever, the Eulerian approach had large correlation coeffi -
cients (>0.99) showing that this method can predict relative 
hemolysis, which would be useful in comparative analysis, 
for example, for ranking different devices or for design opti -
mization studies. ASAIO Journal 2012; 58:363–372.
Damage to the red blood cell (RBC) with resulting release 
of hemoglobin into the blood plasma is known as hemolysis. 
This blood cell trauma manifests as morphologic changes, 
shortened life span, biochemical functional alterations, 
and complete rupture. Mechanical shear stress is known to 
cause hemolysis either by exerting sufficient stress to com -
pletely rupture the RBC membrane or through formation of 
pores in the RBC membrane when the stress/strain exceeds 
a critical value. In addition to the magnitude of the shear 
stress, hemolysis is also increased by increasing duration of 
the RBC’s exposure to shear stress. 1–3 Shear stress below a 
critical level results in either elastic reversible elongation of 
the cells, if applied for a short time, or nonreversible shape 
change with longer application. The former conditions do 
not create hemolysis. However, the latter one may yield 
hemoglobin leakage into the blood plasma. 4
Two major types of models for hemolysis have been proposed: 
strain-based models5–7 and power law models. Strain-based mod-
els aim to model the deformation of the RBCs and corresponding 
strain of the cell membrane and calculate the subsequent hemo-
globin released, a bottom up approach. In contrast, power law 
models are derived from the empirical observation that under 
constant uniform shear stress the increase in plasma free hemo-
globin, ∆Hb, is a power law function of shear stress and exposure 
time, a top down approach. Power law models have been the 
most popular, probably because of their ease of implementation 
and apparent broad applicability. The first power law model was 
developed by Giersiepen et al.,8 for use in calculating the hemo-
lysis index (HI), due to mechanical heart valves, and was based 
on in vitro hemolysis data obtained from a Couette shearing 
device in which human RBC suspension was exposed to shear 
stresses, , (<255 Pa), for exposure times, t (<700 msec):

Over the last decade there have been persistent attempts 
to use computational fluid dynamics (CFD) for predicting 
hemolysis.11 In general, there are two types of computational 
methods available: Eulerian and Lagrangian approaches. In the 
Eulerian approach, the damage index is integrated over the 
entire computational flow domain whereas in the Lagrangian 
formulation the integration is along the flow path lines.

Although hemolysis has been extensively studied both exper-
imentally and computationally, there is still a debate on deter-
mining the most appropriate and universal modeling approach. 
Furthermore, there is less evidence of experimentally verified 
computational models. In this article, the HI predictions of five 
Lagrangian approaches (HI1, HI2, HI3, HI4, and HI5) and the 
Eulerian method with scalar transport (E), were evaluated and 
compared with measurements in two physical devices. The HI 
calculations were performed considering three different sets of 
constants for the power law equation as given in Table 1. The 
computational evaluation was carried out in an axial shearing 
device, which has a uniform shear region in the narrowest gap 
between the rotating spindle and the outer housing surfaces. 
This satisfies the experimental conditions of Equation 1. The 
evaluation was further extended to a clinical VAD in which 
there are complex ﬂow and shear fields.

Meshing
The 3D geometry of the Hemolyzer-H and the CentriMag 
blood pump were generated using the 3D computer-aided 
design software package SolidWorks v2007 (SolidWorks 
Corporation, Concord, MA). The geometries in Parasolid for -
mat were imported into the grid-generation software Gambit 
v.2.3.16 (Ansys Fluent Inc., Lebanon, NH). The ﬂow domain 
of the Hemolyzer-H was meshed using 2.7 million hexahedral 
elements.
The axial position of the magnetically levitated impeller 
within the CentriMag pump is determined by the balance 
between magnetic and lift forces. This position was measured 
experimentally for each operating condition, and separate 
meshes were created for each condition. Each mesh used the 
same controlled hybrid (hexahedral and tetrahedral) structure 
(Figure 2). Hexahedral cells were used for the inlet and outlet, 
core, and gap regions. The rest of the geometry was meshed 
with uniform size (3 mm) tetrahedral elements. The overall size 
of the model was 3 million cells.
Flow Calculations
The computational domain of the Hemolyzer-H is com -
posed of the ﬂuid, spindle surface, and the inner surface of 
the outer housing. No-slip boundary conditions were applied 
at the walls: rotational speed was specified for the spindle and 
zero velocity for the outer wall. Uniform velocity inlet and 
outﬂow outlet conditions were used and the inlet and outlet 
were extended so the solution was not constrained by these 
boundary conditions. Reynolds number (Re) was 20–220 at 
the inlet and 85 in the gap; therefore, laminar solutions were 
calculated.
The computational domain of the CentriMag blood pump 
is composed of ﬂuid, the impeller/rotor surface, and the inner 
surface of the outer housing. The ﬂuid domain was divided into 
two sections: rotating ﬂuid and stationary outer ﬂuid. The rota-
tional motion of the impeller/rotor section was defined with 
the multiple reference frame (MRF), which is a steady-state 
approximation of the rotational motion. No-slip boundary 
conditions were applied at the walls, uniform velocity at the 
inlet, and uniform pressure at the outlet. Re at the inlet was 
2100–5000, and therefore turbulent solutions were simulated. 
The SST-k turbulence model was used as it has been found 
to give good predictions of the pressure head in a similar cen-
trifugal pump.15
The commercial finite-volume CFD solver Fluent (Fluent 
v6.3.26, Ansys Fluent Inc., Lebanon, NH) was used to solve the 
ﬂow field. The semi-implicit method for pressure-linked equa-
tions pressure-velocity coupling algorithm was used, and all 
equations were discretized with second order accuracy. Flow 
convergence was monitored with the pressure head values as

Hemolysis Calculations
The Eulerian hemolysis model was programmed and com -
piled into the CFD solver using user-defined functions and 
user-defined scalars (UDS). Once the ﬂow solution was 
obtained, the scalar transport equation was solved. Conver -
gence was monitored by the UDS residuals (<10 −7) and sta -
bilized predictions at the exit of the device. The HI values for 
each computational cell were calculated by postprocessing 
the data with a separate user-defined code. To obtain the final 
HI, the mass-weighted average of the values at the exit of the 
device was calculated.
For the Lagrangian models, the path lines were obtained by 
postprocessing the ﬂow solutions. Parameters for calculating 
the path lines were chosen so as to get as many path lines to 
reach the exit as possible. There were between 68 and 1974 
path lines for the Hemolyzer-H and between 1165 and 1456 
for the CentriMag blood pump. The HI calculations used Mat-
lab (Matlab 6.5.1, The MathWorks Inc. Natick, MA). All the 
computations were carried out on a PC workstation with two 
Intel Xeon quad-core 2.66GHz CPUs and 16.0 GB RAM with 
Windows XP 64 bit operating system.

Shear stress field. The uniformity of the shear stress field in 
the gap region of the Hemolyzer-H was assessed by examining 
shear stress as contour plots and along path lines ( Figure 4). 
The CFD-predicted mean scalar shear stress in the gap region 
varied between 203 and 208 Pa, as the ﬂow rate was increased 
from 10 to 100 ml/min, which is in good agreement with the 
analytical value (198 Pa), calculated using the rotational speed, 
radius of the spindle at the gap region, ﬂuid viscosity, and the 
thickness of the gap ( tm=⋅ ⋅/H9275ra / ). At 400 ml/min the CFD-
estimated mean scalar shear stress was 264 Pa. The difference 
between computational and analytical values is caused by the 
axial component of velocity, which creates a parabolic pro -
file imposed on the larger linear profile and results in slightly 
higher shear stresses at the walls.
Small regions of high-shear stress were found upstream and 
downstream of the gap. The shear stress in these spots is sig -
nificantly higher than the shear stress in the gap (up to 338 
Pa). However, as their volumes are localized and extremely 
small, their effect on the total blood damage is assumed to be 
small. Shear stress along the path lines (Figure 4B and C)  is 
higher inside the gap region than upstream or downstream; 
however, there are spikes in the shear stress plots in the 
upstream and downstream regions because of the presence 
of the high-shear spots. At 100 ml/min the path lines spent 
an average 1.3 msec within these high-shear spots compared 
with 17.6 msec in the gap.
HI Predictions. The HI predictions (Figures 5 and 6) from six 
different calculation approaches (five Lagrangian and the Eul -
erian) each with three sets of power law constants, along with 
the experimental results, are presented in Figure 6 for vary -
ing ﬂow rate conditions at a constant rotational speed of 8000 
rpm. The calculations with GW constants overestimated for 
every HI calculation method, but were closest with HI2 (mean 
absolute error compared with experiments 0.24%, percentage 
error 1360%) compared with experimental values. Note that 
many predictions exceeded the scale of the graph. With HO

the experimental results (mean absolute error compared with 
experiment is 0.013%, percentage error 54%); whereas Eule -
rian, HI3, HI4, and HI5 methods overestimated the results.

Discussion
Reliable prediction of hemolysis is a key step to designing 
more biocompatible blood-contacting devices. In this work, 
we compared one Eulerian approach and five Lagrang -
ian approaches for CFD-based prediction of hemolysis in 
blood-contacting devices. We also compared the impact of 
three different sets of power law constants reported in the 
literature. The numerical predictions were compared against 
experimental measurements conducted on a custom-made 
blood shearing device with a uniform high-shear stress field, 
and a clinical VAD with a complex shear stress field. The 
mesh convergence studies and comparison of the pressure 
differences across the two devices showed that the calcula -
tions were satisfactory for the analysis presented given the 
limitations that are outlined in the ensuing sections. The 
mean error, mean percentage error, and correlation coef -
ficient for each model and the combined data from both 
devices, are given in Table 3.

the Lagrangian methods, which rely on seeded path lines 
to sample the ﬂow, the whole ﬂuid domain is included in 
the calculation. For the Hemolyzer-H device, the HI con -
tours obtained at different ﬂow rate conditions, with the TZ

The CentriMag HI contours, obtained for 3000 and 5000 
rpm rotational speeds at 5 L/min ﬂow rate, are shown in 
 Figure 8. Again only the calculations with the TZ constants 
are presented. The highest concentration of plasma hemo -

device, which is a result of the secondary ﬂow path. Elevated 
hemoglobin concentration is also seen close to the inclined

zone there (Figure 8, vector plot).

Lagrangian Calculations
The Lagrangian path lines were obtained by releasing 
pseudoparticles from the inlet surface of the correspond -
ing device. The HI accumulations were calculated along the 
particle trajectories or path lines to the exit of the device. 
The number of path lines varied with the device and operat -
ing condition. The Hemolyzer-H with a ﬂow rate of 20 ml/
min used the fewest path lines, 68. To check whether this 
was enough, we performed all the same HI calculations with 
half of these path lines and found percentage differences, 
as compared with the original HI predictions, of between 
0.8 and 3.2%. Also, the variation in the calculated HI in the 
Hemolyzer-H with number of path lines was assessed up to 
104 paths for one operating condition ( Figure 3C ), and the 
calculations were found to be independent of the number of 
path lines above 20 for this relatively simple device. How -
ever, a similar analysis on the CentriMag, with path lines up 
to the original number used, showed that at least 500 were 
required.
As stated by Grigioni21,32 integrating a mini power law does 
not reproduce the uniform shear stress results, and therefore 
HI1 was not expected to produce accurate HI predictions. In 
fact, the HI1 results with HO constants have small errors and 
fairly large correlation coefficients (>0.95). However, results 
for the HI1 depended on the size of the time steps that the path 
line was divided into (Figure 3D), whereas the other Lagrang-
ian methods were independent of the time step. Had a dif -
ferent time step been used for the analysis with the HI1, the 
results would be different.
The calculated HI values with the HI3 accumulation 
method using HO constants provided the smallest relative 
error (57%). However, on average, the HI2 accumulation 
method had the smallest relative error compared with the 
experimental values (as compared with the averages of the 
other methods), but had the smallest correlation coefficients 
between the predicted results and experimental values with 
all three sets of constants. It can be shown that although the 
HI2 accumulation method is correct for a constant uniform 
shear stress it is unlikely to be correct for anything else. For 
example, in a case where a constant shear stress is applied 
for a fixed time interval, and at all other times there is no 
stress, HI2 will depend on the actual time at which the shear 
stress interval starts. Because of the time exponent ( -1) in 
the equation, which is negative for fractional , the later the 
shear stress is applied the lower the predicted hemolysis. This 
explains why, even for the hemolyzer, in which the stress is 
close to uniform, the HI2 results are much lower than those 
of HI3: the stress occurs in the middle of the device rather 
than at the start.
The HI values from HI3 were not inﬂuenced by the time 
step size, or the actual time, and therefore HI3 looks a prom-
ising Lagrangian method. However, the correlation coeffi -
cients are below 0.9 and significantly lower than the Eulerian 
method. Both HI3 and the Eulerian method are based on 
the same damage equation, only the implementation differs. 
We believe the path lines do not fully represent the com -
plicated ﬂow domains studied here. For example, path lines 
initiated from the inlet will never enter a recirculation zone, 
whereas those seeded in a recirculation zone will never exit 
the device.
The HI values calculated with HI4 and HI5 prediction meth-
ods, although not exactly the same, were very close to each 
other, and to HI3. Both HI4 and HI5 methods aim to account 
for the loading history. The reasoning is that exposure to high-
shear stress at a previous time step may result in higher damage 
at the current time step, as compared with exposure to low 
shear stress in the previous time step and the same stress in 
the current time step. Although the two methods differ in their

Limitations
The experimental measurements of hemolysis presented 
here used ovine blood. However, the fragility of RBCs from 
different species is likely to differ33,34 so this validation analysis 
is valid only for ovine blood. In addition, the analysis is limited 
to Couette-type ﬂows and the ﬂow through a specific clinical 
VAD. It is expected that the errors would be similar in other 
similar devices, that is, in other centrifugal VADs, however, fur-
ther studies are required to extend the analysis for other blood-
contacting devices in which the shear stresses and exposure 
times are significantly different.
Blood was treated as a single phase Newtonian ﬂuid. Shear 
rates were high (>100 sec −1) in the vast majority of the blood 
volume (the CentriMag pump has <2% of the volume with 
a shear rate <100 sec −1), which justifies neglecting the shear 
thinning nature of blood. However, blood consists of two 
major phases: plasma and RBCs. In certain situations, the rela-
tive concentrations of the two phases varies, for example, in 
narrow capillary tubes. The gap in the hemolyzer was just 100 
μm, so the concentrations may vary across the gap. The models 
presented cannot account for this.
The MRF method was used to account for the rotation of the 
CentriMag pump impeller. The MRF method is an approxima-
tion that cannot account for transient effects or conditions other 
than steady state. In reality, as the impeller blades pass the outlet 
at the cut-water region there is a ﬂuctuation in the pressure head 
and the ﬂow field, and hence the shear stress field changes.
This work assesses the hemolysis models for reproducing in 
vitro hemolysis measurements and does not attempt to account 
for the complicated nature of ﬂow in vivo. When VADs are 
implanted, the ﬂow through them is determined by the inter -
action of the VAD and the native cardiovascular system and 
therefore has a pulsatile component. In vivo, the plasma free 
hemoglobin is a balance between the amount of hemoglobin 
coming from the RBCs and the amount being processed by the 
kidneys. These hemolysis models cannot calculate the in vivo 
hemolysis.

Conclusions
The comparative numerical and experimental results pre -
sented here demonstrate clearly that the accuracy of power 
law–based hemolysis prediction is inﬂuenced by both the 
power law constants and the numerical approach adopted. 
None of the methods could reproduce the experimental 
results satisfactorily. The Eulerian scalar transport methods, 
although having considerable errors compared with the 
experiments (minimum percentage error = 91%), had high 
correlation coefficients (>0.99), suggesting that this approach 
can be used to give relative comparisons of hemolysis in 
different devices, which would be useful, for example, for 
ranking different devices or for design optimization.
