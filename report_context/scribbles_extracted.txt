Repetitive Hypershear Activates and Sensitizes Platelets in a​
Dose-Dependent Manner​
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4854792/pdf/nihms-769456.pdf 
 
Yes—this paper adds some very relevant discussion, especially around what your 
platelet SA results can actually establish. It contributes more to your report’s 
interpretation and limitations than to your haemolysis equations. 
I read it against haem5.py, your empirical-threshold script, and HAEM_DIAGNOSTICS.md. I 
haven’t seen your actual report, so “new” here means beyond those files. The paper is 
Sheriff et al. (2016), Repetitive Hypershear Activates and Sensitizes Platelets in a 
Dose-Dependent Manner. 
1. Equal stress accumulation does not necessarily mean equal platelet activation. 
Your SA calculation reduces a trajectory to SA=∫τ dt. The paper discusses substantially 
different activation responses at similar SA when stress magnitude, waveform and repetition 
differ. Its comparisons with earlier experiments suggest that intermittent high stresses can 
produce more activation than constant exposure with a similar total dose. PDF pp. 7–8. 
For your report, this supports treating SA as a useful exposure and design-comparison 
metric, rather than a complete biological activation model. Two geometries could have 
similar SA distributions but different peak stresses and exposure patterns. 
For your code, it gives a reason to examine peak stress and stress histories alongside 
SA—much of which your diagnostics already supports. 
2. Platelets can retain a response to previous shear exposure. 
The paper investigates sensitization: platelets exposed to high shear may subsequently 
continue activating in low shear. This is especially relevant when a trajectory ends at your 
device outlet; biological consequences may continue downstream. PDF pp. 6, 8–9. 
However, the single-passage sensitization trend in this experiment was not 
statistically significant. Present it as suggestive evidence and a limitation of your current 
model, rather than a proven outcome for your TAH. 
Your code accumulates exposure but does not model platelet activation state or 
sensitization. That distinction deserves a paragraph in the report. 
3. A single device passage cannot establish the effect of repeated circulation. 
Repeated exposures produced significant activation in the variable-shear experiment. This 
adds biological motivation to the repeated-exposure limitation already acknowledged in your 
empirical screening notes. PDF pp. 6–9, Table 1. 
Two different effects matter: 
●​ Recirculation inside the device can prolong a particular passage. 
●​ Systemic circulation can bring previously exposed platelets back through the device. 


--- PDF PAGE 14 ---
Your streamline analysis may capture the first within its tracking limits; it does not establish 
the second. Multiplying single-pass SA by a passage count would give an exposure 
estimate, but would not reproduce sensitization or recovery between passages. 
4. This paper makes your fixed platelet “threshold” wording too strong. 
The repeated-exposure experiment first found significant PAS elevation at 1611 dyne·s/cm², 
under its particular protocol. That is not a replacement for your 35 dyne·s/cm² Hellums 
reference. The endpoints, blood preparations and exposure histories differ. PDF pp. 5–8. 
More importantly, your code currently describes the Hellums line as a “clinical activation 
threshold” and implies that the fraction exceeding it directly estimates risk. This paper 
supports more cautious wording: 
“Fraction of sampled trajectories exceeding the selected SA reference criterion.” 
That fraction is neither a measured fraction of activated platelets nor a clinical thrombosis 
probability. I would prioritise correcting that interpretation before adding another model. 
5. Platelet activation, platelet lysis and haemolysis are different endpoints. 
The study measures platelet procoagulant activity through a modified prothrombinase assay, 
plus morphology. It does not validate your RBC haemolysis HI/NIH predictions or your 
platelet-lysis boundary. PDF pp. 5, 9. 
Its experiments also used isolated platelets at low concentration, with short intervals 
between repeated exposures. The authors explicitly caution that this differs from 
physiological blood and circulation. Those limitations prevent directly transferring its 
experimental threshold to your CFD results. 
My recommendation: use this paper prominently to discuss the limits of SA, repeated 
exposure, and platelet memory. Keep your existing haemolysis calculations; the most 
immediate improvement is to qualify the platelet threshold language. The key report 
argument is that your analysis compares mechanical exposure, while biological 
thrombogenicity additionally depends on exposure history and the response being 
measured. 
All page references above refer to your local PDF (papers/nihms-769456.pdf).

1. Hemolysis Quantification is Superficial and Indirect 
This is probably your biggest and most exploitable gap. 
The paper uses wall shear stress (Pa) in a simplified tube as a proxy for hemolysis risk, 
with a binary pass/fail against 150 Pa. This misses almost everything about how hemolysis 
actually works in a TAH: 
●​ No Hemolysis Index (HI) is ever calculated. The standard approach uses the 
Giersiepen-Wurzinger power law model: HI% = C × τ^α × t^β, where τ is shear 
stress and t is exposure time. The paper never applies this. 
●​ No conversion to mg/100 L or g/100 L, so there's no comparison possible against 
the NIH threshold (>0.1 g/100 L indicating hemolysis) or against the clinically 
measured 37.15 ± 12.42 mg/100 L from the 50cc SynCardia — which you already 
have from your meeting notes. That's a glaring gap. 
●​ Their 2% shear stress criterion (comparing 35cc to 50cc outlet tube) failed, and they 
simply accepted this because the other criterion passed. There's no discussion of 
what that failure actually means physiologically. 
Your gap to exploit: Compute a proper HI and convert to mg/100 L for direct clinical 
comparison using your CFD data from the actual chamber geometry, not a tube. 
2. CFD is on a Tube, Not the Ventricle Geometry 
The CFD analysis was not performed on the 3D ventricular model — it was done on two 
separate simplified cylindrical tubes representing only the outlet channel. This is a 
fundamental limitation: 
●​ No analysis of internal flow patterns, recirculation zones, or stagnation regions 
inside the blood chamber, which are primary sites of hemolysis and thrombosis in 
pulsatile TAHs. 
●​ The diaphragm dynamics (which drive ejection and filling) are completely absent 
from the fluid analysis. The moving boundary interaction with blood is a known driver 
of hemolysis in pneumatic TAHs. 
●​ No valve hemodynamics — mechanical heart valves (tilting disc or bileaflet) are 
major hemolysis and thrombosis hotspots, and the paper doesn't address them at all. 
●​ Steady-state flow was assumed. A TAH is inherently pulsatile — steady CFD 
misses peak shear stress events during valve opening/closing and rapid ejection 
phases. 
Your gap: Running CFD on the actual 3D chamber with pulsatile boundary conditions and 
moving-mesh or dynamic diaphragm modelling gives you results this paper cannot produce. 
 
3. Two of the Four Engineering Requirements Were Never Verified 
The paper explicitly states ejection fraction and cardiac output verifications were not 
completed. This means:

●​ There is no demonstrated evidence that the 35cc design produces a cardiac output 
in the 2.6–5.4 L/min range for the target age group. 
●​ There is no demonstrated ejection fraction ≥ 0.61. 
●​ The design is declared "feasible" based on only two of four requirements — size and 
a partial hemolysis pass. 
Your gap: Your flow loop directly measures cardiac output, and your CFD can estimate 
ejection fraction. You will be the first to actually validate these for a sub-50cc TAH-like 
device. 
 
4. Cardiac Output is Not Normalized for Pediatric Patients 
The paper cites a cardiac output target of 2.6–5.4 L/min (from Wu et al. 2016), which covers 
a wide age range. For your target population (6–10 year olds): 
●​ Cardiac index (L/min/m²) is the appropriate normalized metric for pediatric patients 
and varies significantly with BSA. A 6-year-old and a 10-year-old have meaningfully 
different requirements. 
●​ Stroke volume is never explicitly verified either — your meeting notes correctly 
identify 30–40 mL as a physiologically grounded target, but this paper doesn't state a 
stroke volume target at all. 
●​ Mean arterial pressure targets are also absent from the verification, despite being a 
direct output of any real flow loop. 
Your gap: Design your loop to target cardiac index per BSA rather than absolute cardiac 
output, and measure MAP as a primary outcome.

This is a textbook gap — computational predictions without experimental validation are
